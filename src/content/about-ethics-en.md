## Reflectiveness

We recognize that knowledge-sharing is never completely neutral, and always involves choices, interpretation, and perspectives. We therefore continuously reflect on our own positions, experiences, and the possible effects of our work.

## Respect for Neurodivergent Lived Experience and Scientific Evidence

We respect the lived experiences of neurodivergent people and recognize them as an important source of knowledge for understanding neurodiversity. We see lived experience and research evidence as complementary sources that can inform and engage with one another.

## Community Participation

We value two-way exchange with community members. We do not see readers as passive recipients of knowledge, but as people who can participate in discussion and learning together.

## Co-creation

**Nothing about us without us.** Discussions, research, and decisions concerning neurodivergent people should, as far as possible, include the participation of neurodivergent people themselves, rather than being defined or spoken for solely by others.

## Language Autonomy

We respect the ways in which individuals and communities choose to name and describe their own experiences. We encourage different languages and concepts to continue developing through dialogue, rather than seeking a single standardized answer.

## Accessibility

We hope to make knowledge, resources, and discussions as accessible to as many people as possible, including accessibility in language, forms of expression, and ways of accessing information.

## Intersectionality

We understand that neurodivergent people are not a single, homogeneous group. Their experiences are shaped by multiple identities, social positions, and environmental factors, including gender, age, culture, language, geographical location, disability, and socioeconomic background. When discussing neurodiversity, we hope to pay attention to these intersecting experiences rather than treating any group as a homogeneous whole.

## Localization

We are interested in how knowledge about neurodiversity is communicated, understood, and developed in Chinese-language contexts. We believe that translation is not simply a matter of converting one language into another, but also involves translating between cultures and contexts. We therefore encourage continued exploration of forms of expression and discussion that are relevant to the Chinese-speaking world, drawing on local experiences, research, and community practices.

## Reducing Stigma, Rather Than Eliminating Pathology

We are committed to reducing misunderstanding and stigma surrounding neurological differences in public discourse, while recognizing that some individuals may genuinely experience difficulties and may need appropriate support.

---

*These principles are not goals that we have already fully achieved. Rather, they are values that we hope to continually practice and reflect on in our work.*
